# Purchase ledger FY2025 — exception review for the controller

The two automated layers produced 39 groups to adjudicate. Nine warrant management attention; the rest are explainable. One item sits outside the flags and is the one I would act on first.

The thing to fix this week is not in the flags at all. One supplier, Kärrenbach Dichtungstechnik GmbH, exists three times in the vendor master (V-0003, V-0004, V-0005) under one VAT number and one Hamburg address, but with three different spellings of the name and, more to the point, three different IBANs. The byte-different names are why the name-hygiene test never saw it. Three masters for one legal entity mean any duplicate-invoice control keyed on vendor id is blind across them, and three bank accounts mean a payment can be routed to any of the three. Combined spend is €49,001, all on account 4110, and the second and third records only came alive in April. I found no realised double payment yet — no matching amount within fifteen days across the three ids — but the exposure is open. Confirm the three IBANs really belong to Kärrenbach, merge the records, and block the duplicates before the next payment run.

Among the rule flags, one classification error is clear and worth correcting: every one of Mainostoimisto Kaarniala's eight advertising invoices (V-0006, memos "Mainoskampanja") was hand-keyed by U-204 to IT services (7200) rather than Marketing (7100), its only permitted account — €36,934 sitting in the wrong line all year. This is the only mapping breach in the whole ledger; recode it and fix the posting default.

Four duplicate groups need an AP check before I would let them rest. Three of them — V-0008, V-0009, V-0010 — share the same signature: each vendor's orders are invoiced twice for the exact same amount within a week, with both invoices quoting the same order number and no partial-delivery marker. That is the pattern the duplicate test exists to catch, and it cannot be cleared from the ledger alone. The recoverable halves are €1,457.70, €5,145.60 and €4,061.10 if goods were received once. The fourth, V-0018, is weaker: two invoices of exactly €7,420.00 four days apart, but under two different purchase orders, so more likely two real orders — still worth confirming the deliveries are distinct.

Two more groups I have marked to verify rather than accept. V-0011's six steel invoices are each an exact thousand (5k, 6k, 7k, 8k, 9k, 6k); per-tonne steel does not land on round thousands six times running, so the amounts look set by hand and should be matched to a purchase order or contract. And V-0012's only four invoices, one per quarter of freight, all fall in €9,580–9,900 — the narrow band just under the €10,000 department-head approval tier, one of them at 9,899.99. Whoever is sizing them, that control never fires on a €38.8k relationship; it is worth confirming the bills reflect actual carriage. Neither is an accusation; both are things the data cannot explain on its own.

A good deal was checked and stood down with figures behind it. The three split-delivery groups (V-0013, V-0014, V-0015) trip the split test by summing just over €10,000, but every line is e-invoiced by the integration user, carries an explicit "osatoimitus" (partial delivery) marker with distinct sub-order numbers, and the parts are unequal mid-range amounts rather than slices engineered to sit under the threshold — genuine staged deliveries. One structural point for you, not a transaction problem: if the €10,000 approval is enforced per invoice rather than per order, honest partial deliveries slip under it too, so the control is worth a look in its own right. The twelve round €15,000 postings (V-0016) are contractual monthly rent from a property company — a round-number false positive. The two weekend postings by U-031 are one warehouse vendor's agreed overtime for a stock count on 15 November, stated plainly in the memo. U-117's eight non-working-day postings are the only real pattern here — that user accounts for nearly all out-of-hours entry while four of the six AP staff have none — but the lines are small, varied and ordinary, so I read it as sanctioned weekend catch-up worth a word rather than an alarm.

The detector layer, on review, added nothing the rules missed. Its 200 flags do not overlap the rule flags at all, none breaches an account mapping, none is a round or near-threshold amount, and none is a duplicate. Every grouped vendor's flags turn out to be its own largest or rare-account invoices, all within the accounts that vendor is allowed and inside its own annual range — including the single highest-scoring row in the ledger (V-0135, a €3,722 telecom invoice on an allowed account) and the vendors whose biggest invoice edges just over €10,000 as one genuine line (V-0099 at €11,282, V-0132 at €10,040). The top-ten singletons and the thirty-row residual tail are the same story at higher and lower scores respectively. I have stood all 26 detector units down individually, each with its numbers, and would not spend AP time chasing them.

Beyond the flags I ran the usual sweeps. Monthly totals by account are stable — the only one-off is a clean €78,400 machinery purchase (V-0019, October, a capital item, not an expense anomaly); manual posting is spread evenly across the six AP users; no invoice predates its vendor's active-from date; and no IBAN is shared across vendors except within the Kärrenbach cluster above.

What I did not look at: only the seven files I was given. I did not open any answer key, design note, decision log or other analyst's work, and I have taken nothing about this ledger from outside those seven files. Everything above is recomputable from the transactions, the vendor master, the calendar and the two flag files.

# Exception review — Saarnitukku Oy purchase ledger, FY2025

Sixty-four rule flags and two hundred detector flags resolve into thirty-nine units. Ten of them warrant action; the rest are explainable. One item outside the flags is worth more of your attention than anything the machines caught.

The single thing to do this week concerns the vendor master, not a posting. One German seals supplier, VAT DE199283746 at Beispielstrasse 14 in Hamburg, exists three times over — V-0003 "Kärrenbach", V-0004 "Kaerrenbach" and V-0005 "KARRENBACH" — and each record carries a different IBAN. The byte-identical name test never fired because the spellings differ by an umlaut and by case. Between them the three records took €48,001 across fifteen invoices, all on account 4110. The amounts do not repeat, so this is not a double payment; the risk is that a single supplier is paid through three master records and three bank accounts, which is precisely the setup payment-diversion exploits. Merge the records to one and confirm which IBAN is genuinely the supplier's before the next payment run.

Three vendors show the fingerprint of duplicate billing. V-0008, V-0009 and V-0010 each have two orders that appear twice at the identical cent amount a few days apart, with no partial-delivery note and no offsetting credit — €842.50 and €615.20 twice over for V-0008, €1,365.00 and €3,780.60 for V-0009, €2,940.75 and €1,120.35 for V-0010. If a single delivery underlies each order, the combined overpayment is about €10,664. A three-way match against goods receipts will settle it. I have separated these from V-0018, whose two equal €7,420 invoices carry two different purchase-order numbers and are therefore two real orders that happen to share a price — stood down.

The €10,000 department-head approval tier is being approached from both sides by four low-volume vendors. V-0012's four quarterly freight invoices all land between €9,580 and €9,899.99, one of them a cent under €9,900; V-0013, V-0014 and V-0015 each post partial-delivery pairs, every part under €10,000 but each pair summing to €11,000–13,300. The partial-delivery memos give an innocent reading, and the ledger as a whole shows no threshold-dodging — fifty-one invoices cross €10,000 openly across fourteen vendors — so I have marked these to watch rather than act. Confirm the underlying order values went through the right approval level. Separately, every one of advertising agency V-0006's invoices, €36,934 of campaign work, is coded to IT services (7200) instead of Marketing (7100) by one clerk; this loses no cash but misstates the accounts, and should be reclassified.

Almost everything the detector raised stands down on the same evidence. Its two hundred flags fall, case by case, on a vendor's rarer-but-allowed account or on its own largest invoices; none sits on a disallowed account, none duplicates a rule flag, and none breaches the approval tier. The same holds for the ten highest-scoring singletons — each a vendor's high-water mark — and for the thirty residual lines, whose scores top out at 0.016. The twelve €15,000 rent postings, the weekend entries that a memo ties to agreed inventory overtime, and one clerk's occasional day-off data entry (eight small invoices in 1,466) are all benign.

What the two layers miss is the top of the ledger. The largest transaction in the book, an €78,400 machinery purchase, is keyed by hand and flagged by neither layer — the rules target specific bands and the detector cannot see a vendor with a single transaction. The next three largest, including a €24,600 insurance premium, are also manual and also unflagged. These look legitimate, but they are the most material items you have and nobody is screening them; a simple value-based review of manual postings above, say, €15,000 would close the gap.

A note on scope. I worked only from the seven supplied files. I did not see purchase orders, goods receipts or payment-clearing records, so the duplicate and split conclusions are inferences from the postings and need those source documents to confirm. The ledger is net of VAT and holds no credit notes, and I did not test VAT or IBAN validity against any registry — that verification is the manual step recommended for the Kärrenbach records.
